Data sources description

ADTC provides four main modules: “LncRNA”, “Alternative Splicing”, “Alternative Polyadenylation”, “RNA Editing”. (1) “LncRNA” module contains three sub-sections: (a) Differential Analysis: Presents ancestry-associated differences in lncRNA expression. (b) Survival Analysis: Allows users to search by lncRNA name to view ancestry-associated survival differences among EUR EAS and AFR across 21 cancer types. (c) LncRNA Drug Target: Enables users to browse imputed response differences for drugs targeting lncRNAs under clinical investigation. (2) “Alternative Splicing” module includes two sub-sections: (a) Differential Analysis: Reports ancestry-related splicing differences. (b) Survival Analysis: Allows users to search by gene name to view ancestry-associated survival differences influenced by AS among EUR EAS and AFR across 21 cancer types. (3) “Alternative Polyadenylation” module includes two sub-sections: (a) Differential Analysis: Displays ancestry-associated APA events. (b) Survival Analysis: Allows users to search by gene name to view ancestry-associated survival differences influenced by APA among EUR EAS and AFR across 21 cancer types. (4) “RNA Editing” module includes two sub-sections: (a) Differential Analysis: Displays ancestry-associated differences in A-to-I RNA editing. (b) Survival Analysis: Allows users to search by gene name to view ancestry-associated survival differences influenced by editing among EUR EAS and AFR across 21 cancer types. In addition to these core modules, the Download page provides access to relevant datasets, and the Document page offers user guidance. ADTC serves as a valuable resource for investigating ancestry-informed differences in transcriptomic profiles and their clinical relevance.

 Data Processing

Identification of lncRNA-related clinical trials

We retrieved JSON-formatted records for all 514,481 clinical trials listed on ClinicalTrials.gov as of November 3, 2024. T To identify trials involving lncRNAs, we searched using a comprehensive set of keywords, including: ‘lncRNA’, ‘long non-coding RNA’, ‘long noncoding RNA’, ‘lncrnas’, 'ncRNA', 'non-coding RNA', 'noncoding RNA', 'long ncRNA', 'lincRNA', 'lncR', and related variants. This search yielded 342 lncRNA-relevant trials. Trial enrollment data were extracted using Python-based parsing tools, and after manual curation, we identified 41 trials specifically focused on 25 lncRNAs. Due to the lack of detailed outcome data on ClinicalTrials.gov, we manually reviewed referenced publications when available. However, no comprehensive clinical outcomes could be obtained.

Data collection of lncRNA expression, PTMs and protein expression

We obtained lncRNA expression profiles from TCGA using the TCGAbiolinks R package (53), with transcript quantification normalized as Transcripts Per Million (TPM), based on GENCODE v36 annotations. For downstream analyses, we retained lncRNAs with a median TPM ≥ 0.1 across samples within each cancer type, designating these as “detectable” lncRNAs (63).

Alternative splicing (AS) data across 8,705 TCGA patients were retrieved from the Pan-Cancer Atlas splicing resource (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/PanCanAtlas-Splicing-2018) (54). We included splicing events only if ≥75% of tumor samples in a given cancer type (40), and ≥10 samples per group at least two ancestral groups, had non-missing percent spliced in (PSI) values.

Alternative polyadenylation (APA) data across 10,537 tumors spanning 32 cancer types were downloaded from the Cancer 3′ UTR Atlas (TC3A; https://www.synapse.org/Synapse:syn24982198/files) (55). APA events were quantified using the DaPars algorithm (https://github.com/ZhengXia/DaPars), which infers proximal polyA sites and calculates the Percentage of Distal polyA site Usage Index (PDUI) per transcript.

A-to-I RNA editing profiles were identified using our previously developed computational pipeline (10), originally applied to 17 TCGA cancer types with ~1.4 million editing sites from the RADAR reference panel (64). This pipeline was later expanded to 33 cancer types using an updated reference panel of ~4.5 million sites (65). RNA-seq BAM files from 10,179 tumor samples were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal, and processed to generate the GPEdit data portal (49). For quality control, we retained editing sites with ≥10 high-quality reads, including at least two reads supporting the editing event. Sites were further filtered using a binomial test (FDR <5%) to retain those with editing frequency >0.1% (66). Here, we used A-to-I RNA editing data released from GPEdit and kept editing sites present in ≥10 samples per group for the following differential analysis.

The latest protein expression data from approximately 8,000 TCGA tumors were obtained from The Cancer Proteome Atlas (TCPA; https://tcpaportal.org/rppa500/download.html) (56). Protein abundance was measured using reverse-phase protein arrays (RPPA), covering 447 clinically relevant proteins.

Differential analysis of lncRNA expression, PTMs and multi-omics data across ancestries

Clinical data (e.g., age at diagnosis, gender, and smoking status) for 32 cancer types were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Tumor purity estimates were obtained from both the TIMER resource (http://cistrome.org/TIMER/download.html) (61) and Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/253193) (62), and then merged. Genetic ancestry assignments were based on a previously published study (5). We focused on 21 cancer types where at least two ancestral groups, European (EUR), African (AFR), and East Asian (EAS), each had ≥10 tumor samples.

Differential analyses were conducted in EUR vs. AFR, EUR vs. EAS, and AFR vs. EAS for lncRNA expression, PTMs (AS, APA, and RNA editing), and protein expression. To minimize confounding effects (e.g., age at diagnosis, gender, smoking status, tumor purity, histological type, and tumor stage), we applied the matching weights (MW) method from the propensity score matching (PSM) algorithm (21,67,68). Balance was evaluated via standardized differences (<0.1 post-adjustment). Statistical significance was determined using the Benjamini & Hochberg to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Criteria for identifying significant ancestry-associated differences were as follows: lncRNA expression: fold change > 1.5 and FDR <0.05 (69); AS: |median percent spliced in (PSI) difference | >0.1 and FDR <0.05 (40); APA: |mean percentage of distal polyA site usage index (PDUI) difference| >0.1 and FDR <0.05 (70); RNA editing: |mean editing level difference| ≥5% and FDR <0.05 (10).

Ancestry-associated differences in mRNA expression, DNA methylation, somatic mutations, and SCNAs across ancestries (EUR vs. EAS, EUR vs. AFR, AFR vs. EAS) in 21 cancer types were previously characterized in our earlier study (7). In the current work, we leveraged these prior results to extract differential data specifically related to AS factors and APA factors. For protein-level analyses, we used the most recently updated protein expression dataset (56), and conducted differential analysis following the same statistical approach as described in our earlier study (7).

All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3.

Survival analysis

Overall survival (OS) data for 21 cancer types were obtained from a previous study (78). For each ancestry group within each cancer type, patients were stratified into high and low groups based on the mean values of the corresponding transcriptomic features, including lncRNA expression, alternative splicing (percent spliced-in, PSI), alternative polyadenylation (percentage distal polyA site usage index, PDUI), and RNA editing frequency. Each group contained at least 10 samples. Associations between transcriptomic features and OS were evaluated using the coxph function in the R “survival” package, adjusting for available covariates such as age at initial pathologic diagnosis, gender, histological type, pathologic stage, tobacco smoking history, and molecular subtype, when applicable. To account for multiple comparisons, results were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, with significance defined at FDR < 0.05. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the R “survminer” package.

Acquisition of clinically actionable genes, alternative splicing factors and APA factors

We compiled a set of 161 clinically actionable genes curated in our previous work (7). We also curated 38 well-established AS factors based on prior literature (31–33). For APA factors, we assembled a set of 34 key genes involved in major regulatory complexes, including CPSF, CFIm, CFIIm, CSTF, PAP, and PAB, guided by previous studies (16,47).

lncRNA-drug response analysis

We obtained the “Curated ncRNAs targeted by drugs” from ncRNADrug (http://www.jianglab.cn/ncRNADrug/download.html) (27), and focusing specifically on lncRNAs targeted by therapeutic agents. From a total of 19,698 curated ncRNA–drug interactions, we identified 1,527 lncRNA–drug pairs in Homo sapiens, comprising 1,343 unique lncRNAs and 75 drugs. To evaluate ancestry-associated differences in drug responses to lncRNA-targeting therapies, we integrated imputed half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values from two large-scale resources: 272 drugs from cancerRxTissue (https://manticore.niehs.nih.gov/cancerRxTissue/) (28) and 251 drugs from VAEN (https://github.com/bsml320/VAEN/) (29). These IC50 values for TCGA patients, widely used in previous studies (21,67,71), were imputed based on drug response and genomics data from GDSC (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer), CCLE (Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia), and TCGA multi-omics datasets (28,29). We harmonized drug names using annotation information from the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). For cancerRxTissue, duplicate drugs were averaged, resulting in 251 unique drugs. There were 143 drugs overlapping between cancerRxTissue and VAEN; thus, we combined IC50 values from both sources to generate a final dataset of 359 drugs (251 from cancerRxTissue and 108 VAEN-specific) for downstream analysis.

To assess differential drug sensitivity across ancestries (EUR vs. AFR, EUR vs. EAS, AFR vs. EAS), we also applied the PSM, adjusting for potential confounders including age at diagnosis, gender, tumor purity, histological subtype, and tumor stage. Only cancer types with at least 10 samples per ancestry group were included. Statistically significant differences in drug response were identified using FDR < 0.05.
